Strong in-domain scores do not establish transferable phishing detection

A focused benchmark tailored to Transforming Incident Response: The Role of Hugging Face Diffusers in Automating Cybersecurity Workflows.

Scope: the paper's proposed model is fine-tuned DistilBERT using Hugging Face Transformers. It is not evaluated here: its fitted checkpoint and exact data are unavailable, and new transformer training was outside the first-pass budget. These are executed baseline results, not a replication or evidence that transformers win or lose.

1. What the paper actually specifies

Sections 4.1–4.5 select DistilBERT for binary alert validation, incident-type prediction and phishing classification, with a separate token-labeling model for threat intelligence. The example uses AutoModelForSequenceClassification, three epochs, learning rate 5×10−5, batch size 16 and weight decay 0.01. BERT-base and RoBERTa are comparators the paper considers; keyword/manual workflows are its operational comparison.

Section 4.2 describes approximately 5,000 private SOC alerts, 2,000 incident descriptions and 10,000 balanced emails combining Enron and phishing collections, with an 80/10/10 split. The exact releases, private labels and trained checkpoint are not supplied. The case study mixes a scripted attack timeline with estimated manual response. Its approximately 45-second removal, 90-second isolation and 10-minute containment figures (pp.17–19) cannot be validated using email classification alone. Its daily precision/recall wording does not provide an unambiguous confusion matrix.

Whitespace/case-normalized full-text hashes remove within-release exact duplicates before splitting and detect cross-release exact overlap (none found). No campaign, near-duplicate or temporal split was possible in this small run. All models receive at most 6,000 characters. Seeds 42/43/44 sample overlapping populations, so split SD is descriptive.

Metric trap: the Nazario-trained majority rule predicts every email as positive, giving 0.539 transfer F1 despite a 100% false-positive rate and macro-F1 0.269. Its apparent transfer lead is not a useful detector. The machine-learned transfer classifiers instead have low false-positive rates but miss most positive examples.

4. Noise and difficulty grid

Models are trained on clean text and evaluated on clean text, 5% or 15% expected alphabetic-character obfuscation (leetspeak or zero-width separators), or only the first 80 words. Both benign and positive emails are perturbed. These are controlled synthetic stressors, not observed attack frequencies.

For word SVM, 15% obfuscation changes mean F1 from 0.994 to 0.985 on Nazario and from 0.947 to 0.925 on MixedEmail. Truncation reduces MixedEmail word-SVM F1 to 0.916 and character-LR F1 to 0.861. Character features do not lead under these particular corruptions. Cross-release mismatch is a much larger descriptive change than these text stressors, but source confounding and positive-label mismatch prevent attributing it solely to a change in phishing tactics.

5. Statistical comparisons: what is supported

On the fixed seed-42 test sets, all 15 model pairs are compared in 10 train/test/setting cells. Exact two-sided McNemar tests compare correctness; Holm adjustment controls the family across all 150 tests. Separately, 1,000 stratified paired bootstrap resamples estimate pointwise 95% intervals for F1 differences. Those intervals are neither multiplicity-adjusted nor tests of the same quantity as McNemar.

On clean MixedEmail, word SVM exceeds character LR by 0.0555 F1 on seed 42 (pointwise bootstrap interval 0.0175 to 0.1066). Six examples are correct only for SVM and none only for character LR: raw McNemar p=0.03125, but global Holm p=1.000. Therefore this small run does not establish a familywise-significant SVM advantage over character LR. For first-80-word MixedEmail, the corresponding Holm p=0.415. Do not interpret unadjusted intervals as adjusted significance.

Inference is conditional on these sampled emails and their labels. Shared campaign templates and source clustering could make sample-level uncertainty too optimistic. No inferential claim is made about the difference between within-release and transfer scores, which use different target samples/labels.

These are computed failure signatures, not manually proven causes. The asymmetric transfer collapse is consistent with narrow source features and a shift from credential lures to a wider phishing/spam label; a source/campaign-controlled annotation audit is needed to separate those explanations. failure_cases.csv in the archive identifies each recorded error by hash, model, condition and truth/prediction, without reproducing sensitive messages or live links. failure_summary.csv provides counts and text features.

7. What was deliberately not claimed

  • No Diffusers experiment: the paper does not specify an executable diffusion-based classification method.
  • No measured transformer ranking: DistilBERT, BERT-base and RoBERTa need a separate training run. An unexecuted DistilBERT extension is included with the paper's stated training settings and independent validation/test handling.
  • No invented human baseline, MTTR gain, analyst workload reduction, or actual containment action. Measured CPU prediction latency in the CSV is not incident-response latency.
  • No NER score: APTNER is a public task-matched candidate for the paper's threat-intelligence component, but its repository permission needs clarification. Network-flow intrusion datasets would not benchmark email-text classification.
  • No synthetic email replacement: suitable public email data exist. Only the stressor corruptions are synthetic, with known unmodified labels assumed invariant.

8. Reproducible handoff

The archive contains executed code, an explicitly unexecuted transformer extension, detailed README, all 180 grid rows, leaderboard, 150 paired tests, hash-only failures, split hashes, training timings and provenance with download URLs, SHA-256 values and core package versions. Full raw emails are not redistributed. Exact hyperparameters, class mappings, limitations and commands are in README.md.

Recommended next experiment: run DistilBERT on these same splits, then repeat with campaign/source-separated data and manually verified phishing-only labels. Use separately calibrated thresholds and report precision at the operational prevalence before considering automated deletion or containment.
